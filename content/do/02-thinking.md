# Practice 2 — I M Thinking

PART A — Learning the Practice  

In the previous practice,  
we learned how to guide the breath—  
how to take a conscious breath  
using a 4-4-4 rhythm.  

As the breath begins to settle,  
attention becomes a little more steady.  
And with that steadiness,  
something else becomes easier to notice.  

The activity of the mind.  

The mind is constantly active.  
It produces thoughts—  

about what has happened,  
what is happening,  
and what might happen next.  

Thoughts move in many ways.  
They revisit the past.  

They anticipate what might come next.  
They react to what is happening right now.  
They create stories,  
interpretations, and possibilities.  

This movement is continuous.  

Most of the time,  
thinking happens automatically.  
And because this process is always happening,  
we rarely notice it.  

We simply follow one thought to the next.  

Research suggests  
that we experience thousands of thoughts each day—  
a constant stream moving through the mind.  

In this practice, which we call I M Thinking,  
we begin to notice this process directly.  

It is not about stopping thoughts.  
It is not about controlling the mind.  

It is about learning to observe thoughts  
as they arise—  
without getting pulled into them.  

To see them,  
instead of becoming them.  

Here is something important to understand.  

The mind is not the source of thoughts.  
It is the instrument through which thoughts take shape.  

Thoughts arise in Awareness—  
the same Awareness you are.  

Because we are  
so used to identifying with the mind,  
we often assume  
that our thoughts are who we are.  

But thoughts appear,  
change,  
and pass.  

Awareness remains.  

Think of the ocean.  

Waves rise and fall on the surface.  
They are always moving.  

But beneath the waves,  
the ocean itself remains steady.  

Thoughts are like waves—  
temporary activity on the surface.  
Awareness is the ocean.  

When we mistake the waves  
for the whole ocean,  
life feels busy and overwhelming.  

When we recognize what lies beneath,  
there is steadiness—  
even as thoughts continue to move.  

To begin working with thoughts,  
we use a very simple support.  

A single, gentle thought.  
Not for its meaning—  
but for its sound.  

The thought we use in this practice  
is So Hum.  

This thought is not something to analyze.  
It is simply a soft mental sound—  
a pattern that aligns with the rhythm of the breath.  

If you listen closely,  
you may notice—  
as air moves in,  
a subtle sound, like so.  

And as air moves out,  
a gentle sound, like hum.  

You don’t need to hear this exactly.  
It is simply a way to notice  
how closely this sound aligns  
with the natural rhythm of breathing.  

In this practice,  
we are not controlling the breath.  
The breath continues on its own.  

The soft mental sound, So Hum,  
is introduced as a light,  
repeating thought in the mind.  

Because it is simple and neutral—  
it becomes an anchor.  

A place for attention to return  
whenever the mind drifts.  

And it will drift.  

That is not a mistake.  
That is the nature of the mind.  

When you notice  
that attention has moved elsewhere,  
you gently return  
to the anchor thought—So Hum.  

Again.  
And again.  

Over time,  
a quiet shift begins.  

You start to notice thoughts  
instead of being carried by them.  

You use a thought  
to become aware of thinking—  

and in that shift,  
begin to recognize the Awareness  
in which thoughts arise and dissolve.  

This is what the practice,  
I M Thinking, develops.  

Not fewer thoughts—  
but a different relationship with them.  

People who practice this often notice:  
greater clarity,  
less reactivity,  
more space in challenging moments.  

Not because thoughts disappear—  
but because they no longer dominate.  

At times,  
the anchor thought may fade.  

There may be moments of quiet.  

That quiet is not empty.  
It is Awareness itself—  
still, present, here.  

This practice is simple—  
and its impact builds quietly over time.  

You don’t need to look for results  
while you’re doing it.  
There is nothing to achieve in the moment.  

Instead, allow the practice to work in the background.  

With regular, daily repetition,  
the shift often becomes visible  
not during the practice—  
but in how you move through your day.  

In how you respond,  
how you think,  
how you handle complexity and pressure.  

So keep it simple.  
Stay consistent.  

Do the practice,  
and allow the experience  
to reveal itself—  
in your everyday life.  

PART B — Daily Practice  

Alright — let's do this.  

Find a comfortable seat,  
or just pause wherever you are.  

(Just not while driving, operating machinery,  
or doing anything that needs your full attention —  
the mind needs a moment of yours.)  

Settle in.  
Let your shoulders drop  
(they're allowed to, even if your inbox isn't).  
Let your face soften.  

Now gently —  
notice your breath.  

We'll start with a few rounds of 4–4–4,  
the same rhythm you know.  

Breathe in —  
one… two… three… four.  
Hold —  
one… two… three… four.  
Exhale —  
one… two… three… four.  

Again. Take your time.  

Breathe in — one… two… three… four.  
Hold — one… two… three… four.  
Exhale — one… two… three… four.  

Good. Now let the breath find its own pace —  
smooth, unforced.  

As it settles,  
the mind starts to settle too.  

Here's where we introduce the anchor —  
**So Hum**.  

Let it arise gently in the mind,  
the way a word does when you remember it.  

No rhythm to follow.  
No need to match the breath.  
It just sits there,  
quietly.  

When other thoughts wander in — and they will —  
just notice them.  
You don't have to greet them, fight them, or follow them.  
They'll move along on their own.  

When you realize attention has drifted,  
that's the moment — that's the practice.  
Come back gently to *So Hum*.  

So… Hum…  
So… Hum…  
So… Hum…  

Keep going at your own pace.  

If attention drifts a hundred times,  
return a hundred and one.  

If the anchor naturally fades  
and the mind becomes still,  
just rest there.  

Nothing to do.  
Nothing to hold.  

Continue gently  
for the next few minutes.  

So… Hum…  

So… Hum…  

Now slowly,  
let go of *So Hum*.  

No need to return to it.  

Let the mind be just as it is —  
no effort, no controlling, no managing.  

Simply rest. Two or three minutes is plenty.  

When you're ready,  
become aware of your body again.  

Notice your breath.  
Let it deepen a little.  

And ease your way back into the day.  

That was something. Well done.
