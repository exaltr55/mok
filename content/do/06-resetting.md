# Practice 6 — I M Resetting

Yousourceful — Practice 6: I M Resetting  

Full Script — Narrator Voiceover  

PART A — Learning the Practice  

[Narrator voiceover. Calm, warm, unhurried tone. Paired with gentle visuals — quiet domestic scenes, hands resting, a phone face-down, a simple meal, natural light.]  

In the practices we have explored so far, we have worked with the breath, the mind, with talking, with writing, and with conscious movement of the body.  

Each of these practices helped us build awareness — awareness in motion, awareness in expression, awareness in the body.  

Now we turn to something equally important, and often quietly overlooked.  

The ability to stop.  

This practice is called I M Resetting.  

And at its heart, it is simply about giving ourselves permission to pause.  

To step away — even briefly — from constant stimulation. From habits that run on their own. From patterns and compulsions that shape our days without us quite noticing.  

Just as we recharge our devices when they run low, our bodies and minds also need regular moments of restoration — to return to balance, to clarity, and to a genuine sense of choice in how we live.  

Resetting works through two simple forms.  

The first is a dietary reset — a temporary, gentle pause from food that allows the body to rest, repair, and rebalance.  

The second is a digital reset — a pause from screens and constant incoming information that allows the nervous system and the mind to settle and recover.  

Each of these can be practiced at two levels.  

A daily mini-reset — short, gentle pauses that fit naturally into everyday life.  

And a weekly deeper reset — a longer pause that allows for a more complete restoration of body and mind.  

The daily reset acts like a tune-up. It prevents overload from accumulating.  

The weekly reset acts like a reboot. It clears accumulated fatigue and restores a sense of equilibrium.  

When practiced regularly, I M Resetting brings real and noticeable benefits.  

Physically, the body gets time to repair and renew. Digestion improves. Sleep deepens. Energy becomes more stable.  

Mentally, the mind grows clearer. Mental fog begins to lift. Focus improves. Attention feels lighter and more spacious.  

Emotionally, there is less reactivity. Less comparison. Less background stress. More calm. More presence.  

And perhaps most importantly, resetting gradually restores a sense of freedom — freedom from compulsion, freedom from automatic habit.  

We begin to notice an urge without immediately obeying it.  

We begin to respond consciously, rather than react automatically.  

That shift — small as it may seem — changes everything.  

At the heart of this practice lies an ancient yogic principle called Pratyahara — the withdrawal of the senses.  

Not suppression. Not denial.  

Simply stepping back — so that we are no longer quietly controlled by cravings, habits, or the relentless pull of stimulation.  

Resetting is not a punishment.  

It is a practice of freedom.  

You may begin with daily resets and explore weekly ones when you feel ready.  

There is no rush. There is no forcing.  

Only conscious pauses — taken gently, practiced with care, and returned to again and again.  

PART B — Practicing I M Resetting  

[Narrator continues. Tone remains warm and practical. Visuals shift to reflect each section — food preparation, a quiet kitchen, a phone set aside, a journal open.]  

Resetting can be practiced at two levels.  

A daily mini-reset — gentle, consistent, and brief.  

And a weekly deeper reset — longer, more complete, and deeply restorative.  

Both can be practiced through food and through screens.  

You don't need to begin with everything at once.  

Start where it feels most natural. Build gradually.  

Consistency matters far more than intensity.  

These conscious pauses are not just about rest.  

They create space — space to notice your patterns.  

When you reach for food. When you reach for your phone. What is actually driving that impulse.  

With awareness, patterns that once felt automatic begin to loosen.  

And over time, what no longer serves you begins to fall away — not through force, but through simple, steady noticing.  

Dietary Reset — Practicing With Food  

A dietary reset is not only about food.  

It is about awareness.  

It brings gentle attention to what you consume, how you eat, and when you eat.  

At times, it may include giving the digestive system a true and complete break.  

When intake pauses, the body can shift from constant processing to something quieter — repair, restoration, and rebalancing.  

Before You Begin  

Take a few moments to set yourself up gently.  

Plan your meals ahead of time. Avoid keeping easy snack triggers close by. Keep water or herbal teas ready. Let family members know, if it helps.  

And set a simple intention — something like:  

"Today, I am giving my body space to reset."  

If you have any medical condition or specific dietary needs, please speak with your physician before beginning.  

Daily 3-Hour Dietary Reset  

This is the simplest place to start.  

If you usually sleep around 10 pm, aim to finish dinner by around 7 pm.  

After dinner, do not eat anything more.  

Allow the body to rest overnight without additional input.  

During this window, avoid late-night snacks, desserts, or calorie-containing drinks.  

You may drink: water, or herbal teas.  

Even this short pause, taken with awareness, can support digestion, deepen rest, and help you wake feeling lighter and more refreshed.  

You may also choose any 3-hour window during the day — though the evening works particularly well as you naturally begin to wind down.  

Weekly 20-Hour Dietary Reset  

This is your deeper reset — a longer pause that allows the body a more complete restoration.  

Choose a day that works for your life.  

A simple structure that works well:  

Stop eating around 7 to 8 pm on Friday. Continue through Saturday morning and into the early afternoon. Break the reset around 4 pm with something light and wholesome.  

During this period, drink water and herbal teas freely.  

If you are new to this practice, or find the pause difficult, you may include very light foods to ease yourself in —  

simple fruit, a light soup, boiled potatoes, or kichri — rice and lentils.  

This practice is not about pushing.  

It is about listening — to what the body genuinely needs, rather than what it habitually craves.  

Staying With the Practice  

In the beginning, you may notice hunger at familiar times of day.  

These are often what we call hunger-clock signals — habit patterns, not true hunger.  

They usually pass within ten to fifteen minutes.  

This is where the practice deepens.  

You begin to see that not every urge is a real need.  

Some are patterns. Some are old conditioning.  

And simply by noticing them — without immediately acting — you begin to gently change them.  

When cravings arise, try quietly reframing:  

"I am choosing freedom, not compulsion."  

You can also support yourself by:  

drinking a glass of water, taking a short mindful walk, or writing a few lines in your journal.  

Your I M Writing practice can be a powerful companion here — helping you stay connected to your intention when the pull of habit feels strong.  

Think of this pause not as deprivation, but as giving your body a holiday.  

Like simple, gentle housekeeping — it clears out the old and creates space for something fresher to arrive.  

Digital Reset — Practicing With Screens  

A digital reset gives the nervous system a break from constant stimulation.  

It allows the mind to slow down, to settle, and to become genuinely clearer.  

Before You Begin  

Prepare gently.  

Let friends, family, or colleagues know. Decide in advance how you will use the time. Silence notifications. Keep devices out of reach.  

On your phone, you may use Focus Mode or Do Not Disturb — allowing communication to pause without effort or constant checking.  

Daily 3-Hour Digital Reset  

Choose a window — ideally from 7 pm until you sleep.  

During this time:  

Turn off televisions, laptops, and phones. Keep handheld devices in another room if that feels helpful.  

Replace screen time with:  

Conversation. Journaling. Reading. A creative hobby. A simple, non-digital game — sudoku on paper, a puzzle, a sketch.  

The mind begins to recognize this window as a signal to slow down — to prepare gently for rest.  

Weekly 20-Hour Digital Reset  

Choose a longer window.  

You may align this with your dietary reset if that feels natural:  

Friday 8 pm to Saturday 4 pm, or Saturday evening to Sunday afternoon.  

If a full reset feels like too much at first, start smaller — 4 waking hours, then gradually extend toward the full 20.  

There is no failure here. Only steps.  

During the Reset  

Switch devices off completely, or keep them on airplane mode for emergencies only.  

Fill the time with genuinely nourishing activities:  

Journaling reflections. Mindful walks. Cooking with awareness. Meditation or simple rest. A creative hobby. Meeting a friend in a calm, unhurried place.  

This is not empty time.  

It is restorative time — and it will often feel more alive than you expected.  

Returning From the Reset  

When you return to screens, do so slowly.  

Begin with something uplifting.  

Avoid moving straight back into news, social media, or anything demanding.  

Keep the rest of the day light on digital consumption — to preserve the clarity the reset has created.  

Staying With the Practice  

You may notice what we might call the phantom phone reach.  

The hand moves almost automatically — before the mind has even decided.  

When it happens:  

Pause. Smile gently. Notice it.  

That moment of noticing is the practice working.  

Each time you catch the impulse and pause before acting, you are gently retraining the mind.  

Old patterns begin to loosen. Space begins to open.  

And with that space, comes something valuable — genuine choice.  

A small thing that helps:  

Keep the phone out of sight. Place it in a drawer. Or add one small, simple barrier — even putting it in another room is often enough to interrupt the automatic reach and create a moment of conscious choice.  

Reflection During Resetting  

Journaling can deepen awareness during both resets.  

You may use these simple prompts as gentle check-ins throughout the day:  

In the morning:  

"What cravings or impulses am I noticing? How do I usually respond to them?"  

At midday:  

"Without food or screens, what thoughts or feelings are surfacing?"  

In the afternoon:  

"What feels lighter right now? What clarity is beginning to emerge?"  

There are no right answers.  

Only noticing. Only returning. Only the quiet practice of seeing clearly.  

A Few Helpful Notes  

Dietary resetting is not recommended for those who are pregnant or breastfeeding, or for those recovering from illness.  

Expect cravings and some restlessness — especially at the beginning.  

They will pass.  

Use each urge as a gentle reminder:  

"I am choosing freedom over compulsion."  

Resetting works best when it is gentle, regular, and honestly adapted to your life.  

You may also adjust this practice with guidance from a healthcare provider or Ayurveda practitioner if that feels right for you.

with greater clarity, a little more balance, and a gentleness toward ourselves that grows with every conscious pause.  

This is the practice of resetting.  

Simple. Gentle. And quietly powerful.  

Return to it often.
