# Practice 5 — I M Moving

Part A — Learning the Practice

In the previous four practices,  
we worked with the breath,  
the mind, our inner voice,  
and how we process experience through writing.

Each one helps Awareness  
move closer to everyday life.

In I M Moving,  
we bring Awareness  
fully into the physical body.

Movement is not just exercise.  
It is how the body stays alive,  
responsive, and balanced.

For most of human history,  
movement was woven naturally  
into daily life —  
walking, bending,  
lifting, squatting, stretching.

The body stayed mobile  
without planning or effort.

Modern life has changed that.

Long hours of sitting,  
screen-based work,  
and limited physical variation  
have made stillness the default.

Over time,  
this lack of movement  
affects not only the body,  
but energy, mood, and clarity.

When the body doesn't move,  
tension accumulates.  
When movement returns,  
something releases.

I M Moving is a return —  
to how the body is meant to move.

The practice is gentle,  
adaptable across different bodies  
and life stages,  
and repeatable without strain.

There is no ideal version to achieve.

In I M Moving,  
we work with three simple forms:  
postures, walking, and squatting.

Each addresses a different aspect of moving.

The timing is flexible. The frequency is yours.  
What carries the practice is consistency.

## Yoga Postures — Asanas

The word "asana" comes from Sanskrit.  
At its simplest, it means seat or posture.

In yoga, it means a conscious way  
of placing and holding the body  
with steadiness and ease.

Yoga has been practiced  
for thousands of years —  
a path of eight interconnected limbs,  
or aspects of living well.  

Asanas are one of those limbs.

They were never meant  
to be about performance,  
flexibility,  
or achieving a particular shape.

They are about making the body  
a stable, comfortable place  
to live in —  
so that movement, rest,  
and stillness all feel  
more natural.

When we move regularly  
and intentionally,  
we support the systems  
the body relies on —  
circulation, digestion,  
the nervous system,  
the muscles and joints  
that hold us upright each day.

When we don't,  
stiffness accumulates.  
Tension settles.  
Posture shifts in ways  
we rarely notice —  
until something begins to ache.

Asanas offer a gentle,  
consistent way to restore  
what daily life gradually takes away.

This is not a fitness class.  
Not about pushing limits.  
Not about measuring progress  
through difficulty.

It is a practice of Awareness —  
noticing how you hold your body,  
how you respond to discomfort,  
and how small, consistent movements  
create real and lasting change.

You do not need to be flexible.  
You do not need prior experience.  
You only need to show up —  
and pay attention.

In Part B,  
we walk through ten postures together.

The sequence begins gently,  
builds gradually,  
and ends in rest.

## Mindful Walking

Walking is something most of us do  
without thinking.

We move from place to place —  
focused on where we're going,  
rarely aware of the act of walking itself.

Mindful walking is different.

It is the simple practice  
of bringing full attention  
to the experience of walking —  
the sensation of each step,  
the rhythm of the breath,  
the contact of the foot  
with the ground.

It is not about pace,  
or distance,  
or steps counted.

It is about being present  
with each step.

Just a few minutes of mindful walking  
can shift how the day feels.

It can be done indoors or out.  
Slow and intentional,  
or at your natural pace.  
For a few minutes,  
or longer.

In Part B,  
we'll walk together in two ways —  
slow and intentional first,  
then at your natural pace.

## Squatting

The squat is one of the most  
fundamental human movements.

For most of history,  
humans squatted to rest,  
to eat,  
to gather,  
to work close to the ground.

The body knew the shape  
without thinking about it.

We sit in chairs rather than squatting.  
We use lifts rather than lowering ourselves.  
We avoid getting down to the floor.

The body adapts.  
Hips tighten.  
Ankles stiffen.  
Knees lose range.

The squat is not a gym exercise.  
It is a return to a position  
the body once knew well.

Practiced regularly,  
it restores hip mobility,  
ankle flexibility,  
core strength,  
and connection to the ground.

You do not need to squat low.  
What matters is the movement —  
not the depth.

Even five squats a day,  
done with attention,  
build something real over time.

In Part B,  
we'll practice a simple squat sequence,  
gently and slowly,  
with the option of a chair  
for support.

## Together, the three

Yoga opens what tightens.  
Walking restores rhythm.  
Squats rebuild ground connection.

Each on its own is enough.  
Together,  
they offer the body  
a complete return.

Even ten minutes  
of any one of them,  
practiced consistently,  
changes how you move and feel  
over time.

Part B — Doing the Practice

## Before You Begin

A few small things — and you're set.

### What you'll need

A mat or a soft carpeted floor.  
(The kitchen floor will work in a pinch. We've all been there.)

Loose, comfortable clothes  
you can actually move in.

Bare feet, if that feels right.

A clear space —  
enough room to stand, kneel,  
and stretch your arms out  
without redecorating the living room.

A wall or sturdy chair nearby  
for balance support  
(very helpful for tree pose and squats).

### When to practice

Not within two hours of a meal.  
Your body moves better on a lighter stomach —  
and your stomach will thank you too.

Mornings often work beautifully —  
the body has rested,  
and the day hasn't yet shaped it.

But honestly — any time you'll *actually do it*  
is the right time.

### A quick safety note

If you have an injury, chronic pain,  
or any condition affecting movement or balance —  
please check with a healthcare provider before starting.

And throughout:  
move within your comfort range,  
modify or skip what doesn't feel right,  
and stop if anything hurts.

Your body's signals are smarter than any instruction here.  
Listen to it.

yoga postures,

So you can watch the movements  
alongside reading the instructions.

For now,  
follow the written guidance,  
move slowly,  
and let your breath set the pace.

### Pick one for today

You don't need to do  
all three today.

Choose the one  
your body asks for —  
yoga, walking, or squats.

That's enough.

## Yoga Postures

Welcome to the mat.

First time? Welcome.  
Coming back after years off? Welcome.  
Been doing this since you were six? Also welcome.  
This practice meets you wherever you are.

We'll move through ten postures together — slowly, gently.  
No headstands. No human pretzels. Nothing extreme.

Just you, your body, and your breath.

If anything doesn't feel right —  
your knees, your back, your patience —  
ease out. Rest a beat. Come back when you're ready.  
Pausing is part of the practice, not a failure of it.

### 1. Cat-Cow — Spinal Warm-Up

Come down onto your hands and knees.

Hands directly under your shoulders.  
Knees directly under your hips.

Spread your fingers wide.  
Press through the base of each finger.  
Let the whole hand connect  
evenly with the mat.

As you inhale —  
let your belly soften toward the floor,  
your chest open forward,  
your tailbone gently lift.

This is Cow.

As you exhale —  
round your spine upward,  
chin draws toward your chest,  
belly gently hugs inward.

This is Cat.

Flow between the two with the breath.

Inhale — open, soften, lift.  
Exhale — round, curl, release.

Continue for several rounds  
at your own pace.

Notice the warmth beginning to build.  
A little more ease in the back  
with each cycle.

Come back to a neutral spine  
and pause for a breath.

### 2. Mountain Pose — Standing Awareness

Slowly walk your hands back,  
press into the mat,  
and rise up to standing.

Feet about hip-width apart.

Press all four corners of each foot  
into the ground:  
base of the big toe,  
base of the little toe,  
both sides of the heel.

Let the legs engage gently —  
not gripped, just lightly active.

Soften the knees —  
not bent, not locked.

Lengthen the spine upward.  
Imagine the crown of your head  
being drawn gently  
toward the ceiling.

Relax your shoulders.  
Let them drop away from your ears  
and soften slightly back and down.

Arms hang naturally,  
palms facing inward.

Let the breath be full and easy.

Inhale — feel a sense of lift  
move through the whole body.  
Exhale — feel yourself settle,  
ground, and arrive.

Three breaths here.

### 3. Tree Pose — Balance and Focus

Stay where you are.

Wobbling is completely normal.  
It is the practice itself.

Balance is not about being perfectly still —  
it is about learning to return,  
gently, without frustration.

If a wall or railing is nearby,  
place one hand on it.

Shift your weight slowly  
into your right foot.  
Press all four corners down.  
Feel it root.

Now lift your left foot.  
Place it on the ankle,  
the calf, or the inner thigh —  
wherever feels stable today.

Avoid placing the foot  
directly on the knee joint —  
above or below the knee, always.

Bring your hands together at your heart,  
or rest them on your hips.

Find one still point in front of you  
and let your gaze rest there softly.

Breathe slowly and steadily.  
Your body will sway.  
Let it. Just keep coming back  
to that point.

Stay for a few slow breaths.

Release. Both feet down.  
Take one full breath  
before switching sides.

Now root into the left foot.  
Right foot finds its place.

Soft gaze. Steady breath.

Return to Mountain Pose.

### 4. Standing Forward Bend — Release

From standing,  
soften your knees gently.

Take a breath in.

As you exhale,  
hinge forward from your hips —  
not from your waist.

Let the upper body hang completely.

There is no need to reach the floor.  
Hands can rest on your shins,  
your knees,  
or simply hang in the air.

Let the head be heavy.  
Let the neck release fully.

With each inhale,  
feel a little length  
come into the spine.

With each exhale,  
allow the body to soften —  
not forcing, simply allowing.

If your lower back feels tight,  
bend your knees generously.

Stay for a few breaths.

To come back up:  
bend your knees,  
place your hands on your thighs if it helps,  
and slowly roll up through the spine —  
one vertebra at a time.

Head comes up last.

Pause at the top.  
Take a breath.

### 5. Downward-Facing Dog — Full-Body Stretch

From standing,  
hinge forward,  
place your hands on the mat,  
and step or walk your feet back  
until your body forms an inverted V.

Hands shoulder-width apart.  
Feet hip-width apart.

Press firmly through all ten fingers —  
especially the base of the index finger  
and thumb.

Keep a generous bend in your knees  
and focus first  
on lengthening the spine.

Let the hips reach up and back.  
Think of one long line  
from your hands to your tailbone.

Once you have that length,  
slowly begin to straighten the legs —  
only as far as the spine stays long.

Your heels do not need  
to touch the floor.

With each exhale,  
consciously draw the shoulders  
away from the ears.

Stay for a few steady breaths.

Gently lower your knees to the mat  
and rest for a moment.

### 6. Cobra Pose — Gentle Back Opening

From your knees,  
lower your whole body slowly  
down to the mat.

Lie on your belly.

Let your legs relax behind you,  
hip-width apart.

Place your hands flat on the mat  
just below your shoulders.

Elbows stay close to your ribcage.

As you inhale,  
begin to gently lift  
your chest forward and up.

Use your back muscles to initiate the lift —  
not your arms.

Your arms are there  
for light support,  
not to push you up.

Keep the elbows slightly bent.  
Keep the shoulders away from the ears.  
Keep the lower back long.

If you feel any pinching  
in the lower back,  
lower yourself down by an inch or two.

The right height is where the back  
feels open, not strained.

Breathe steadily.  
Don't hold the breath at the top.

On your next exhale,  
slowly lower back down.  
Rest your forehead gently  
on your hands.

### 7. Child's Pose — Rest and Reset

From lying on your belly,  
press your hands into the mat  
and gently push your hips  
back toward your heels.

Let your torso fold forward naturally.

Rest your forehead on the mat —  
or on your stacked hands  
if the floor feels too far.

Your arms can extend forward  
or rest softly alongside your body.

If your hips don't reach your heels,  
place a folded blanket  
between your thighs and calves  
for support.

Close your eyes if that feels comfortable.

With each inhale,  
notice the back of your body expanding.

With each exhale,  
let the body release a little more  
weight into the mat.

There is nothing to do here.  
It is pure rest —  
and rest is part of the practice.

Stay for several breaths.

### 8. Seated Spinal Twist

When you're ready,  
bring yourself up to seated.  
Extend both legs in front of you.

Sit tall —  
let the spine lengthen upward  
from the base.

If your lower back tends to round  
when you sit on the floor,  
sit on a folded blanket or cushion.

Bend your right knee  
and place your right foot flat  
on the mat,  
crossing over your left leg.

Inhale first. Grow tall through the spine.  
Create as much length as you can  
before you rotate.

As you exhale,  
begin to gently rotate to the right.

Place your left elbow or forearm  
against the outside of your right knee  
as a gentle anchor —  
not a lever.

Let the rotation begin  
from the base of the spine  
and travel upward.  
The neck and head follow last.

Keep your chin level throughout.  
The twist is in the spine,  
not the neck.

Each inhale — a little more length.  
Each exhale — a little more rotation,  
softly.

Stay for a few steady breaths.

On an inhale,  
slowly unwind.  
Come back to center.  
Pause and breathe.

Now the other side —  
left knee bends,  
foot crosses over the right leg.

Inhale to lengthen.  
Exhale to rotate gently to the left.

Return to center.

### 9. Butterfly Pose — Hip Opening

Stay seated.

Bring the soles of your feet together  
and let your knees fall open  
naturally to the sides.

Draw your feet in toward your body  
as close as feels comfortable  
without strain.

Hold your feet or ankles —  
whichever allows you  
to sit tall more easily.

Lengthen through the spine  
before anything else.

Do not push or press your knees  
toward the floor.  
That works against the pose.

Let the knees be where they are.  
Gravity and time will do the work —  
your job is to relax and breathe.

With each inhale,  
lengthen the spine upward.  
With each exhale,  
allow the hips to soften —  
just a little.

If you'd like to add a forward fold,  
hinge gently from the hips,  
not the waist,  
and let the torso  
move forward slowly.

Keep the spine long as you fold.

Stay for a few breaths.

### 10. Corpse Pose — Savasana

Gently extend your legs out in front of you.  
Lie down slowly onto your back.

Let your arms fall naturally  
by your sides,  
palms facing up,  
a little away from the body.

Let your feet fall open naturally.

If your lower back feels uncomfortable,  
place a rolled blanket  
or pillow under your knees.

Close your eyes.

Take one deliberate breath in through the nose —  
and let it go slowly through the mouth.

Now let the breath return  
to its natural rhythm.  
Don't control it.  
Just observe it.

With each exhale,  
feel the body grow a little heavier.  
Let the mat hold you completely.

When the mind plans or reviews,  
simply return to the feeling of the body.  
The weight of the legs.  
The rise and fall of the chest.

This is where the practice integrates.  
Where the body quietly absorbs  
everything you've just done.

Stay here for one to two minutes.

When you're ready,  
begin to deepen your breath.

Wiggle your fingers and toes slowly.  
Roll gently to one side  
and pause there.

Then, in your own time,  
press yourself back up to seated.

### Closing the yoga sequence

Take a moment  
before you move on.

Notice how you feel —  
in your body,  
your breath,  
your mind.

More open, perhaps.  
More settled.  
A little more present  
than when you began.

You don't need to do  
the full sequence every time.

Some days,  
a few postures are enough.

What matters most  
is that you return —  
consistently,  
gently,  
without pressure on yourself.

## Mindful Walking

Indoors or outdoors. Quiet street or your own kitchen.  
Doesn't really matter.

You don't need much:  
a small path, a hallway, a corridor —  
anywhere you can walk back and forth for five to ten steps.

Comfortable clothes. Comfortable shoes.  
Or bare feet, if the floor's welcoming.

That's it. No app to pair, no GPS to wait for.

### Slow, intentional walking

Stand still for a moment.

Feel your feet under you.  
Notice your weight across both of them —  
that's gravity doing its job. Thanks, gravity.

Take three slow breaths. Let the body settle.

Now begin to walk —  
much, *much* more slowly than usual.

Notice the lifting of one foot.  
The shift of weight. The placing.  
The other foot lifting now.

If your mind wanders off (it will),  
just come back to the next step.

Walk five to ten steps. Turn. Walk back.

There's nowhere to get to.  
The walking *is* the practice.

Continue for two to three minutes.

### Walking at your normal pace

Now pick up your usual pace —  
the way you'd walk through your house  
or down a familiar street.

This is the harder one, actually —  
keeping attention on the body  
while moving like a normal human.

Notice the rhythm of your steps.  
The light swing of your arms.  
The breath, flowing.

Thoughts will arrive. Just let them pass through.  
Come back to the body in motion.

Continue for two to three minutes.

### Closing the walk

Come to a stop.

Stand still for a moment.  
Feel the body that just walked.  
Notice the breath. Notice the attention.

A few minutes of mindful walking  
shifts something. Small, but real.

You can return to this anytime —  
between meetings, down a hallway,  
walking to the kitchen for water.

The walking was always there.  
*Attention* is the thing we're adding.

## Squats

The squat is a movement  
the body once knew well.

We're not trying  
to reach a specific depth  
or perform a perfect form.

We're simply  
returning to a position  
the body has always understood.

Stand with your feet hip-width apart  
or slightly wider.

Toes pointing slightly outward —  
about ten to fifteen degrees.

Press all four corners of each foot  
firmly into the ground.

Let the arms hang at your sides  
or place your hands  
on your hips.

If a chair is nearby,  
you can use it for balance  
or to squat toward.

Take one full breath here  
before beginning.

### The squat itself

As you inhale,  
lengthen the spine upward.

As you exhale,  
begin to bend your knees  
and lower your hips down  
and back —  
as if you're sitting into a chair.

Keep your chest lifted.  
Keep your spine long.

Let the knees track  
in line with your toes —  
not collapsing inward.

Lower only as far  
as feels comfortable today.

If a chair is behind you,  
let your hips touch it lightly,  
then come back up.

Pause at the bottom for a breath.

As you inhale,  
press through your feet  
and rise back to standing.

Notice the engagement  
of the legs and the core.

That is a squat.

### A few rounds

We'll do five together — slow and steady.

Inhale — lengthen.  
Exhale — lower down.  
Pause at the bottom for a breath.  
Inhale — rise back up.

Five times.

Move slowly. Listen to your body.

If five feels like plenty — stop there. No medals here.  
If you've got more in you — do a few more. The legs love it.

### Closing the squats

Stand for a moment.

Feel the legs. Feel the breath.  
Notice the warmth that's spreading.  
(Yes — that's blood flow. Your hips just woke up.)

Even five squats a day, with attention,  
keep the body strong and the hips mobile.

Do them anywhere —  
during a work break,  
while waiting for the kettle,  
before bed.

Your body has always known this shape.  
You just gave it a moment to remember.

## To close

Whichever section you chose today —  
yoga, walking, or squats —  
your body just received some real attention.

*That* is the practice.

Move tomorrow if you can.  
Skip a day if life happens.  
Come back when you come back.

The path is long.  
What matters is that you keep returning.
