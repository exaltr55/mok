Section 1 — Yoga Postures (Asanas)  

NARRATOR: Educational Context  

Introduction to Asanas?  

The word asana comes from Sanskrit language — and at its simplest, it means seat or posture.  

But in yoga, it means something broader: a conscious way of placing and holding the body with steadiness and ease.  

Yoga, as it has been practiced for thousands of years, is described as having eight limbs — eight interconnected aspects of living well.  

Asanas are one of those limbs.  

They were never meant to be about performance, or flexibility, or achieving a particular shape.  

They were designed to help the body become a stable, comfortable place to live in — so that movement, rest, and stillness all feel more natural.  

Why Movement Matters  

The body is built to move.  

Not to perform — but to function.  

To sit without strain. To stand without effort. To carry, reach, bend, and rest with ease.  

When we move regularly and intentionally, we support the systems the body relies on — circulation, digestion, the nervous system, and the muscles and joints that hold us upright each day.  

When we don't, stiffness accumulates. Tension settles. Posture shifts in ways we rarely notice — until something begins to ache.  

Asanas offer a gentle, consistent way to restore what daily life gradually takes away.  

What This Practice Is — and Isn't  

This is not a fitness class. It is not about pushing limits or measuring progress through difficulty.  

It is a practice of awareness — of learning to notice how you hold your body, how you respond to discomfort, and how small, consistent movements can create real and lasting change.  

You do not need to be flexible to begin. You do not need any prior experience. You do not need to look a certain way or reach a certain depth in any posture.  

You only need to show up — and pay attention.  

How to Approach What Follows  

The sequence you're about to practice moves through ten postures.  

It begins gently, builds gradually, and ends in rest.  

Each posture has a purpose. Your instructor will guide you through each one — what to do, how to breathe, and what to notice along the way.  

A few things to keep in mind:  

Move at your own pace. Let your breath guide you — not the clock, not comparison.  

If something feels uncomfortable, ease out. Rest. Modify. Your body's signals are always worth listening to.  

And finally — this practice works best when it becomes a habit. Not a long one. Not a demanding one. Just a regular one.  

Even ten minutes, practiced consistently, will change how you move and feel over time.  

Now, let's begin.

Welcome.  

I'm glad you're here — whether this is your very first time on a mat, or you're returning after a long break, or yoga has been part of your life for years.  

This practice is designed for all of you.  

We're going to move through ten postures together. Nothing extreme. Nothing to force.

Let's begin.  

1. Cat–Cow Pose — Spinal Warm-Up  

Come down onto your hands and knees.  

Take a moment to set yourself up well. Hands directly under your shoulders — not too far forward, not tucked back. Knees directly under your hips.  

Spread your fingers wide. Press through the base of each finger — let the whole hand connect evenly with the mat.  

Now let's move with the breath.  

As you inhale — let your belly soften toward the floor, your chest open forward, your tailbone gently lift. Allow the head to follow naturally — no need to force the chin up.  

This is Cow.  

As you exhale — round your spine upward toward the ceiling, chin draws toward your chest, belly gently hugs inward. Let the movement begin from the tailbone and travel all the way up.  

This is Cat.  

Now let's flow between the two.  

Inhale — open, soften, lift. Exhale — round, curl, release.  

One thing to watch here: try not to rush through the movement. The breath should lead — the body follows. If you find yourself moving faster than your breath, slow down.  

Also, keep the arms straight but not locked. A soft elbow is fine — just don't let them collapse.  

Continue for a few more rounds at your own pace.  

...  

Notice the warmth beginning to build. A little more ease in the back with each cycle.  

That's exactly what we're looking for.  

Come back to a neutral spine and pause here for a breath.  

2. Mountain Pose — Standing Awareness  

Slowly walk your hands back, press into the mat, and rise up to standing.  

Take your time getting there.  

Once you're up, bring your feet about hip-width apart — roughly in line with your hip bones, not your outer hips.  

Before anything else — just feel your feet.  

Press all four corners into the ground: the base of the big toe, the base of the little toe, and both sides of the heel.  

From that foundation, let the legs engage gently — not gripped or tense, just lightly active.  

Soften your knees — not bent, just not locked. Let your pelvis sit in a neutral position — not tucking under, not tipping forward.  

From there, lengthen the spine upward. Imagine the crown of your head being drawn gently toward the ceiling.  

Relax your shoulders — let them drop away from your ears and soften slightly back and down. Arms hang naturally, palms facing inward.  

A common thing I see here: people either hold their breath or breathe very shallowly. Let the breath be full and easy in this pose.  

Inhale — feel a sense of lift move through the whole body. Exhale — feel yourself settle, ground, and arrive.  

Three breaths here.  

This may look like you're simply standing still. But this is presence — and it's the foundation of everything that follows.  

3. Tree Pose — Balance and Focus  

Stay where you are.  

We're going to work on balance now — and I want to say upfront: wobbling is completely normal. It's not a sign you're doing it wrong. It's the practice itself.  

Balance is not about being perfectly still — it's about learning to return, gently, without frustration.  

If you have a wall or a railing nearby, place one hand on it.  

Shift your weight slowly into your right foot. Press all four corners down. Feel it root.  

Now lift your left foot. Place it on the ankle, the calf, or the inner thigh — wherever feels stable today.  

One important thing: avoid placing the foot directly on the knee joint. The knee isn't designed to take that lateral pressure. Above or below — always.  

Bring your hands together at your heart, or rest them on your hips.  

Now find one still point in front of you — something that isn't moving — and let your gaze rest there softly. This is called a drishti — a focal point. It does more for your balance than almost anything else.  

Breathe slowly and steadily. Your body will sway. Let it. Just keep coming back to that point.  

Stay for a few slow breaths.  

...  

Release. Both feet down. Take one full breath before we switch sides.  

Now root into the left foot. Right foot finds its place.  

Soft gaze. Steady breath.  

...  

Well done. Come back to Mountain Pose.  

4. Standing Forward Bend — Release  

From standing, soften your knees gently.  

Take a breath in — and as you exhale, hinge forward from your hips — not from your waist.  

This is an important distinction. Many people round immediately from the mid-back. Instead, think of leading with your chest first, then letting the body follow as you fold down.  

Let your upper body hang completely.  

There is no need to reach the floor. Hands can rest on your shins, your knees, or simply hang in the air — all of it is right.  

Let the head be heavy. Let the neck release fully — no holding tension there.  

With each inhale, feel a little length come into the spine. With each exhale, allow the body to soften just a little more — not forcing, simply allowing.  

If your lower back feels tight or pulling, bend your knees generously. This is not a hamstring stretch — it's a release. Bent knees are always welcome here.  

Stay for a few breaths.  

...  

To come back up: bend your knees, place your hands on your thighs if that helps, and slowly roll up through the spine — one vertebra at a time.  

Head comes up last.  

Pause at the top. Take a breath. Let any light-headedness settle.  

5. Downward-Facing Dog — Full-Body Stretch  

From standing, hinge forward, place your hands on the mat, and step or walk your feet back until your body forms an inverted V shape.  

Hands shoulder-width apart, feet hip-width apart.  

Press firmly and evenly through all ten fingers — especially the base of the index finger and thumb. This protects the wrists and keeps the weight distributed well.  

Now, rather than trying to straighten your legs right away, keep a generous bend in your knees and focus first on lengthening the spine.  

Let the hips reach up and back. Think of creating one long line from your hands to your tailbone.  

Once you have that length, you can begin to slowly straighten the legs — but only as far as the spine stays long. The moment the back rounds, you've gone too far.  

Your heels do not need to touch the floor. Let them hover if needed — that's completely fine.  

A common mistake here is letting the shoulders scrunch up toward the ears. With each exhale, consciously draw the shoulders away from the ears — broad across the upper back.  

Inhale — feel the length from hands to hips. Exhale — soften the shoulders, relax the neck.  

Stay for a few steady breaths.  

...  

Gently lower your knees to the mat and rest for a moment.  

6. Cobra Pose — Gentle Back Opening  

From your knees, lower your whole body slowly down to the mat.  

Lie on your belly.  

Take a moment to settle here. Let your legs relax behind you, hip-width apart.  

Place your hands flat on the mat just below your shoulders — not out to the sides, not too far forward. Elbows stay close to your ribcage.  

Before you lift, take one full breath in this position. Feel the belly press gently into the mat on the inhale.  

As you inhale — begin to gently lift your chest forward and up.  

Here's what I want you to focus on: use your back muscles to initiate the lift — not your arms. Your arms are there for light support, not to push you up.  

Keep the elbows soft — slightly bent is ideal. Keep the shoulders away from the ears. And keep the lower back long — this is not about going as high as you can.  

If you feel any compression or pinching in the lower back, lower yourself down by just an inch or two. The right height is where the back feels open, not strained.  

Breathe steadily here — don't hold the breath at the top.  

Inhale to stay long. Exhale to soften while still holding the shape.  

...  

On your next exhale, slowly lower back down. Rest your forehead gently on your hands.  

7. Child's Pose — Rest and Reset  

From lying on your belly, press your hands into the mat and gently push your hips back toward your heels.  

Let your torso fold forward naturally.  

Rest your forehead on the mat — or on your stacked hands if the floor feels too far.  

Your arms can extend forward, long in front of you, or rest softly alongside your body — whichever feels more restful right now.  

If your hips don't reach your heels, that's completely fine. Place a folded blanket between your thighs and calves for support if you have one nearby.  

Close your eyes if that feels comfortable.  

Now let's use the breath intentionally here.  

With each inhale, notice the back of your body expanding — the back ribs, the lower back, the space between the shoulder blades.  

With each exhale, let the body release a little more weight into the mat. Feel the forehead soften. Feel the jaw unclench.  

There is nothing to do in this posture. It is pure rest — and rest is part of the practice.  

Stay here for several breaths. There is genuinely no rush to move on.  

...  

8. Seated Spinal Twist  

When you're ready, slowly bring yourself up to a comfortable seated position.  

Extend both legs out in front of you.  

Sit tall — let the spine lengthen upward from the base. If your lower back tends to round when you sit on the floor, sit on a folded blanket or cushion. It makes a real difference.  

Now, bend your right knee and place your right foot flat on the mat, crossing it over your left leg.  

Let the left leg stay extended, or bend it in if that's more comfortable.  

Before you twist — inhale first. Grow tall through the spine. Create as much length as you can before you rotate.  

As you exhale — begin to gently rotate to the right.  

You can place your left elbow or forearm against the outside of your right knee to guide the rotation. But use it as a gentle anchor — not a lever to force yourself deeper.  

A common mistake is twisting from the shoulders and neck first. Instead, let the rotation begin from the base of the spine and travel upward — the neck and head follow last.  

Each inhale — find a little more length. Each exhale — invite a little more rotation, softly.  

Keep your chin level throughout. The twist is in the spine, not the neck.  

Stay for a few steady breaths.  

...  

On an inhale, slowly unwind. Come back to center. Pause and breathe.  

Now the other side — left knee bends, foot crosses over the right leg.  

Inhale to lengthen. Exhale to rotate gently to the left.  

...  

Return to center. Well done.  

9. Butterfly Pose (Baddha Konasana) — Hip Opening  

Stay seated.  

Bring the soles of your feet together and let your knees fall open naturally to the sides.  

Draw your feet in toward your body — as close as feels comfortable without strain.  

Hold your feet or your ankles — whichever allows you to sit tall more easily.  

Now, lengthen through the spine before anything else. Sit up from the base — don't collapse through the lower back.  

Here's something important: do not push or press your knees toward the floor. That's one of the most common things I see, and it works against the pose.  

Simply let the knees be where they are. Gravity and time will do the work — your job is to relax and breathe.  

With each inhale, lengthen the spine upward. With each exhale, allow the hips to soften — just a little.  

If you'd like to add a forward fold: hinge gently from the hips — not the waist — and let the torso move forward slowly. Keep the spine long as you fold.  

Some of you will feel a deep stretch in the inner thighs and hips. Others may feel very little. Both are equally valid — every body is different, and this pose changes over time.  

Stay for a few breaths.  

...  

10. Corpse Pose — Savasana  

Gently extend your legs out in front of you. Lie down slowly onto your back.  

Take a moment to position yourself comfortably.  

Let your arms fall naturally by your sides — palms facing up, a little away from the body. Don't tuck the arms in — give them space.  

Let your feet fall open naturally. No need to hold them together.  

If your lower back feels uncomfortable, place a rolled blanket or pillow under your knees.  

Close your eyes.  

Take one deliberate breath in through the nose — and let it go slowly through the mouth.  

Now let the breath return to its natural rhythm. Don't control it. Just observe it.  

With each exhale, feel the body grow a little heavier. Let the mat hold you completely.  

A common tendency here is to keep the mind busy — planning, reviewing, thinking ahead. When you notice that happening, simply return to the feeling of the body. The weight of the legs. The rise and fall of the chest. That's enough.  

This is where the practice integrates. Where the body quietly absorbs everything you've just done.  

Stay here for one to two minutes.  

...  

When you're ready — begin to deepen your breath. Wiggle your fingers and toes slowly. Roll gently to one side and pause there.  

Then, in your own time, press yourself back up to seated.

Mindful Walking  

What Is Mindful Walking?  

Walking is something most of us do without thinking.  

We move from place to place — focused on where we're going, rarely aware of the act of walking itself.  

Mindful walking is different.  

It is the simple practice of bringing full attention to the experience of walking — the sensation of each step, the rhythm of the breath, the contact of the foot with the ground.  

It transforms an ordinary, automatic movement into a moment of presence.  

An Ancient Practice, An Everyday Application  

Mindful walking has roots in contemplative traditions that go back thousands of years.  

In many of these traditions, walking was considered as valuable a practice as seated meditation — a way of cultivating awareness while remaining active in the world.  

Today, we understand this differently but arrive at the same insight:  

Moving the body with awareness is not separate from mental clarity. It supports it.  

Why It Matters  

Most of us spend the majority of our day either sitting or moving on autopilot.  

Both have a cost.  

Prolonged sitting allows stiffness to build, circulation to slow, and mental energy to flatten.  

Moving without awareness keeps the body busy but rarely brings it back to ease.  

Mindful walking addresses both.  

It gets the body moving — which supports circulation, releases physical tension, and prevents the stiffness that accumulates through long periods of stillness.  

And it brings the mind back — out of thought, out of distraction, and into direct contact with the present moment.  

Even a few minutes can shift your state noticeably.  

How to Approach It  

Unlike the yoga postures in Section 1, mindful walking is not a single session you complete once a day.  

It is a practice you return to many times throughout the day — woven into the natural movement that is already part of your life.  

Short walks are just as valuable as longer ones.  

You can practice mindful walking in the hallways of your office, home, around your neighborhood, on a treadmill, while climbing stairs, or simply moving between rooms.  

You do not need a special place. You do not need a special time.  

You only need to bring your attention to the experience of walking as it is happening — one step at a time.  

The section that follows will guide you through exactly how to do that.

That's completely fine.  

You don't need to slow down to practice mindful walking.  

Simply bring your attention to your feet — at whatever pace you're moving.  

Heel. Sole. Toes.  

The awareness matters more than the speed. That is always true.  

Pausing Along the Way  

After two or three minutes — or whenever it feels natural — come to a pause.  

Stand still for a moment.  

Take two slow breaths.  

Feel your feet on the ground again.  

Notice how the body feels after moving — a little warmer, perhaps. A little more present.  

Then, when you're ready, begin again.  

When the Mind Wanders  

At some point — probably quite soon — the mind will wander.

That is completely natural. That is what minds do.  

When you notice it — and noticing is itself a moment of awareness — simply return.  

Come back to the feet. Heel. Sole. Toes.  

No frustration needed. No judgment.  

Each time you return is the practice working.

Stand quietly for a moment.

A little clearer, perhaps. A little more settled.  

That shift — however subtle — is real.

Section 3 — Squats  

PART ONE — NARRATOR: Educational Context  

[Narrator voiceover. Calm, measured tone. Paired with gentle visuals — people moving naturally, sitting, standing, lifting, close-ups of feet and legs in motion.]  

What Are Squats?  

The squat is one of the most fundamental human movements.  

Before chairs existed, humans squatted to rest, to work, to eat, to tend to the ground.  

It is a movement the body was designed to perform — naturally, repeatedly, and with ease.  

In modern life, most of us have lost regular access to it.  

We sit in chairs rather than squatting. We use lifts rather than lowering ourselves. We avoid getting down to the floor because getting back up has become harder.  

Over time, the muscles and joints that support this movement become underused — and what was once natural begins to feel difficult.  

Why It Matters  

The squat is not a gym exercise.  

It is a functional movement — one that underlies dozens of everyday actions we may take for granted:  

Sitting down into a chair and standing back up. Picking something up from the floor. Stepping in and out of a car. Lowering yourself to tend to a garden, a child, or a pet.  

When the body can perform this movement well, these actions feel easy and secure.  

When it cannot, they begin to feel effortful — and over time, uncertain.  

Practicing squats regularly helps maintain the strength, stability, and confidence that these everyday movements require.  

What This Practice Is — and Isn't  

This is not about depth. It is not about weight or repetition.  

It is about reintroducing a natural movement to the body — gently, consistently, and with awareness.  

You do not need to squat low. You do not need to feel a burn.  

You only need to move within a comfortable range, with attention to how the body feels as you do so.  

If you have existing knee, hip, or back concerns, this practice can still be for you — with appropriate support and modification, which your instructor will guide you through.  

What matters most is not how deep the squat is.  

It is that you return to the movement — regularly, gently, over time.  

How to Approach What Follows  

Your instructor will guide you through a simple squat practice.  

Before you begin, find a space where you have room to stand with arms extended in front of you.  

A chair nearby is helpful — not because you will need it, but because having it there gives you confidence to move freely.

Think of it as the body quietly preparing itself for movement.  

Hand Position  

For this practice, we will use one simple hand position:  

Extend your arms forward, roughly at shoulder height.  

This helps balance the body as you move through the squat.  

If that feels uncomfortable for your shoulders, you can bring your palms together at your chest instead.  

Either is fine. Choose one, and keep it consistent through the movement.  

Before the First Squat  

Before we move, take one slow breath in.  

And one slow breath out.  

Feel the feet. Feel the length in the spine. Feel the body ready.  

Let the movement begin from a place of steadiness — not effort.  

The Squat — Going Down  

As you inhale —  

send your hips back, as if a chair is just behind you and you are about to sit into it.  

Allow the knees to bend naturally. Let them follow the direction of your toes — not caving inward, just tracking forward and out.  

Keep the chest lifted. Keep the spine long.  

Think of the movement happening in the hips first — hips back, then down — rather than just bending the knees.  

Lower only as far as feels comfortable.  

You do not need to go deep. Even a small range of movement is meaningful and valuable.  

At the bottom, pause for just a moment.  

Notice your feet still grounded. Notice the body balanced. Notice the breath.  

The Squat — Coming Back Up  

As you exhale —  

press firmly through all four corners of both feet.  

Feel the floor push back.  

Slowly rise back to standing — legs straightening, chest staying lifted, spine staying long.  

At the top, keep a soft bend in the knees.  

Don't lock them out — just let them stay gently active, joints protected.  

Take a breath here.  

Notice how that felt.  

Finding the Rhythm  

Now let's find a rhythm together.  

Inhale — hips back, lower slowly. Pause at the bottom. Exhale — press through the feet, rise slowly. Soft knees at the top.  

Inhale down. Exhale up.  

Move at your own pace. Let the breath lead.  

If at any point you feel discomfort in the knees, hips, or lower back — reduce the depth, or come out of the movement entirely.  

There is no version of this practice that requires pain.  

Continue for five to eight repetitions — or however many feel right for you today.  

...  

Modifications — Meeting Your Body  

Before we do a second round, I want to mention two options that make this practice more accessible.  

Using a chair: If you have a chair behind you, you can squat toward it — lowering until you just touch the seat, then rising back up.  

The chair gives you a target and removes any fear of going too low. It is not a shortcut. It is a smarter way to move.  

Using a wall: You can also place one hand lightly on a wall beside you.  

Just a fingertip touch for balance — it changes everything if steadiness is a concern.  

Use whatever support allows you to move with more confidence and ease.  

That support is part of the practice — not separate from it.  

Second Round  

Take a breath.  

Let the body settle for a moment.  

When you're ready — feet grounded, chest lifted, arms forward —  

begin again.  

Inhale — hips back, lower slowly. Exhale — press through the feet, rise.  

Five to eight repetitions. Or fewer, if that is what the body needs today.  

Move slowly. Move with awareness.  

...  

Closing the Practice  

When you finish your last repetition, come to standing.  

Let your arms rest by your sides.  

Take two slow breaths.  

Feel both feet on the ground.  

Notice the warmth in the legs — a gentle aliveness that wasn't quite there before.  

That is the body responding. That is strength being maintained.  

CLOSING — Instructor  

Squats are one of those movements that quietly support everything else.  

Every time you sit down and stand up — that is a squat. Every time you reach toward the floor — that is a squat.  

When you practice this movement regularly, those everyday actions become easier, more confident, more secure.  

You don't need to do many. You don't need to push deep.  

You only need to return — gently, consistently, over time.  

Even five squats a day, practiced with awareness, is enough to make a real difference in how the body moves and feels.  

Think of this as a daily investment in your own capability.  

Small. Steady. Lasting.  

Well done today.
